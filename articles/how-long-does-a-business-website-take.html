Website planning

How long does a business website take?

The honest answer depends less on page count than on decisions, content, integrations, review cycles, and launch risk.

A small website with approved content and one decision-maker can move quickly. A redesign involving positioning, many stakeholders, a content migration, CRM workflows, and regulated review cannot responsibly follow the same schedule. A useful timeline begins by identifying the work that controls it.

Discovery and decision-making

The team needs agreement on audience, goals, services, brand constraints, required pages, calls to action, integrations, editing needs, and launch responsibilities. Skipping these decisions rarely saves time; it moves them into design and development, where changing direction costs more.

Content is often the critical path

Writing, collecting proof, selecting imagery, reviewing claims, and obtaining legal or leadership approval can take longer than implementation. Decide who owns each page and schedule content reviews early. Real content should influence design before the site is nearly finished.

Custom design requires focused feedback

Design time depends on the number of distinct page patterns, brand maturity, interaction needs, and review rounds. Feedback moves faster when it is consolidated, tied to the agreed audience and goals, and delivered by the people who have authority to decide.

Development complexity is not page count alone

A website with ten informational pages can be simpler than a three-page site with conditional forms, account behavior, complex animation, several systems integrations, and unusual content editing. Reusable components accelerate work when the content actually shares a pattern.

Redesigns include migration work

An existing site brings content inventory, URL mapping, redirects, analytics history, domain and hosting access, search considerations, and sometimes a CMS migration. Those tasks are part of the product, even though they are less visible than the new design.

Testing and launch need protected time

Allow time for content review, responsive behavior, keyboard and accessibility checks, forms, integrations, metadata, structured data, redirects, analytics, consent behavior, performance, and launch ownership. A deadline that removes this phase is transferring risk into production.

What helps a project move

  • One accountable client decision-maker
  • A realistic sitemap and phased scope
  • Content owners assigned at the start
  • Consolidated feedback on an agreed schedule
  • Early access to domains, hosting, CRM, analytics, and other dependencies
  • A launch date based on readiness and business events, not optimism alone

Use phases when the date is fixed

If the business must launch around an event, campaign, or transition, define the smallest complete first release and schedule lower-priority content or functionality afterward. A deliberate phase is safer than calling unfinished work complete.

Ask for a timeline tied to assumptions

A responsible proposal should explain what is included, which inputs the client owns, how reviews work, what dependencies could change the schedule, and how scope changes are handled. That is more useful than a date presented without conditions.
